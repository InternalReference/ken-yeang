## ⚡ QUICK REFERENCE — the twelve things to get right

*(Full evidence for each is below. Section references in brackets.)*

1. **Bioclimatic design is a SUBSET of ecological design** — his own words, confirmed three times
   (2022, 2026, and the structure of his 14 principles). Do not lead with "bioclimatic". [C.4, D.6]
2. **Biointegration has THREE aspects: physical, systemic, temporal.** Triple-verified across 2008,
   2009 and 2021. Safe to print. [A.4, B, J.6]
3. **There are FIVE design modes, not four:** Passive → Mixed → Full → Productive → **Composite**.
   The four-mode version is a secondary-source simplification. [B.1]
4. **The four ecoinfrastructures have TWO valid naming systems, both his** — colours
   (green/blue/grey/red) for masterplanning; descriptive (nature-based / hydrological / technological
   / anthropocentric) for theory. Unified in the 2020 book as the **"quatrobrid"**. Do not cross-map
   the definitions between them. [A.3, C.1, G.1, J.6]
5. **Ecomimesis is biomimicry at ECOSYSTEM scale — he does NOT reject biomimicry.** [D.3, G.2]
6. **The vertical corridor is the theoretical core of a green tower** — planting must be CONTINUOUS,
   "from the foundations to the green gardens on the rooftops". Fragmentation kills ecology, which is
   why isolated green buildings fail in his terms. [A.6, D.1 Principle 5]
7. **He calls the skyscraper "probably the most ecologically unfriendly of all building types"** and
   defends it only on grounds of necessity. Quote it WHOLE. [K.1]
8. **Health IS in his framework** — Principle 12 of 14 (2026), and the 2021 Dilani book. But it
   arrived VIA Dilani; it was not native to his early theory. [D.1, J, §15]
9. **He ranks decarbonisation BELOW ecology, explicitly and on the record.** [E, D.6]
10. **He publishes almost no numbers — for carbon OR biodiversity.** That is the accurate critique.
    [§16]
11. **His lineage: McHarg (method), Whitehead (process philosophy), von Bertalanffy (systems).** [C.2]
12. **The best numbered manifesto is the 2026 fourteen principles** (Principle / Goal / Design). The
    2007 "premises" paper is paywalled and its list was NOT obtained — do not substitute. [D.1, K.3]

## SOURCE A — "STRATEGIES FOR GREEN DESIGN", Ken Yeang (© 2009)

**This is the single most valuable primary source found so far.** It is a paper authored by Yeang
himself, copyright 2009, distributed via the CIB / IRBnet construction research archive. It contains
his four ecoinfrastructures, his three strands of biointegration, his ecomimesis definition and his
critique of LEED/BREEAM — all in his own words, in one document.

- URL: https://www.irbnet.de/daten/iconda/CIB17235.pdf
- Byline on the paper: "Ken Yeang (© 2009)", contact given as Principal Architect, T. R. Hamzah &
  Yeang Sdn. Bhd., Malaysia.
- The paper closes with his own credential line: "Ken Yeang, DMPN, PhD (Cantab.), A A Dipl.,
  D. Lit. (Hon.), FRSA, APAM, FSIA, RIBA, ARAIA, Hon. FAIA, Hon. FRIAS, Director of Llewelyn Davies
  Yeang (UK) and of T. R. Hamzah & Yeang (Malaysia), Distinguished Plym Professor University of
  Illinois, Adjunct Professor at the University of Malaya, University of Hawaii at Manoa, University
  of Tongji (Shanghai)."

> **NOTE ON QUOTING FROM THIS SOURCE.** The PDF is a scan/typeset with broken intra-word character
> spacing (it extracts as "de sign", "infrast ructure", "com prehensive"). All quotes below have had
> that extraction artefact repaired ONLY by closing those spurious spaces. No words have been added,
> removed or reordered. Two genuine typos in the original are preserved and marked [sic]:
> "BREAM" (for BREEAM) and "itrs" (for "its").

### A.1 The structure of the paper — his own numbered argument

The paper is organised as seven numbered points. Points 2–6 present five design strategies. This is
the closest thing to a compact numbered manifesto found so far. Structure, verbatim headings:

1. "Saving our environment is the most vital issue that humankind must address today, feeding into
   our fears that this millennium may be our last"
2. "The first design strategy is to view green design in terms of weaving of four strands of
   infrastructures" — **the four ecoinfrastructures**
3. "The second design strategy is to regard green design as the seamless and benign environmental
   biointegration of the artificial (the human made) with the natural environment" — **biointegration**
4. "The third design strategy is to regard green design as 'ecomimesis'" — **ecomimesis**
5. "Fourthly, ecodesign can be regarded not only as the creating of new artificial 'living' urban
   ecosystems … but also as one of restoring existent impaired and devastated ecosystems" —
   **restoration and the ecological nexus**
6. "The fifth strategy for ecodesign is to regard our designed system in the context of the biosphere
   globally as a series of interdependent interactions" — **biosphere-scale monitoring**
7. Closing argument: green design must go beyond LEED/BREEAM; "ecological design is still very much
   in its infancy".

### A.2 Green design is NOT engineering, and NOT a rating system (opening framing)

**[YEANG, 2009]**
> "Green design is not just eco-engineering. These are indeed important part of green design (see
> 'grey ecoinfrastructure' below) and are rapidly developing and advancing systems towards a green
> built environment and architecture but not exclusively."

**[YEANG, 2009]**
> "Green design is also not just about rating systems (such as LEED or BREAM [sic], etc.). These are
> useful checklists but are not comprehensive. They are useful as a list of reminders of some of the
> key items to consider in green design and useful in proselytising green design to a wider audience."

**[YEANG, 2009]**
> "The contention here is that achieving effective green design is not as easy as it had been
> contended. It is complex."

Note his stated goal in this paper is not "zero carbon" or "net zero" but: "to arrive at the goal of
achieving a state of stasis with the natural environment."

### A.3 THE FOUR ECOINFRASTRUCTURES — verified definitions

**[YEANG, 2009]** the single-sentence definition of all four, verbatim:
> "The first design strategy is to view green design in terms of weaving of four strands of
> infrastructures: the 'grey' (the engineering infrastructure being the eco sustainable engineering
> systems and utilities), 'blue' (water management and closing of the water cycle by design with
> sustainable drainage), 'green' (the green ecoinfrastructure or nature's own utilities) and 'red'
> (our human built systems, spaces, hardscapes, society and regulatory systems)"

**[YEANG, 2009]** on how they combine:
> "Green design is the blending of all these four sets of infrastructures into a system seamlessly."

**GREEN (nature) — [YEANG, 2009]:**
> "The green infrastructure is the ecoinfrastructure that is vital to every masterplan. This
> ecoinfrastructure parallels the usual grey urban infrastructure of roads, drainage systems and
> utilities. This is an interconnected network of natural areas and other open spaces that conserves
> natural ecosystem values and functions and sustains clean air and water. It also enables the area to
> flourish as a natural habitat for a wide range of wildlife, and delivers a wide array of benefits to
> humans and the natural world alike, such as providing a linked habitat across the landscape that
> permits bird and animal species to move freely."

> "This ecoinfrastructure is nature's functioning infrastructure (parallel to our human-made
> infrastructures, designated as grey, blue and red infrastuctures [sic] here), and in addition to
> providing cleaner water and enhancing water supplies, it can also result in some, if not all, of the
> following outcomes: cleaner air; a reduction in heat-island effect in urban areas; a moderation in
> the impact of climate change; increased energy efficiency; and the protection of source water."

**THE PRIORITY RULE — this is the load-bearing postulate. [YEANG, 2009]:**
> "This ecoinfrastructure take[s] precedence over other engineering infrastructures in the masterplan."

and, the sharpest sentence in the paper:

> "Without it, no matter how clever or advanced is the eco-engineering gadgetry used, the masterplan
> remains simply a work of engineering, and can in no way be called an ecological masterplan or,
> neither in the case of larger developments, an ecocity."

**GREY (engineering) — [YEANG, 2009]:**
> "The grey infrastructure is the usual urban engineering infrastructure such as roads, drains,
> sewerage, water reticulation, telecommunications, and energy and electric power distribution
> systems. These engineering systems should integrate with the green infrastructure rather than
> vice-versa, and should be designed as sustainable engineering systems."

(Note the explicit subordination: grey serves green, "rather than vice-versa".)

**BLUE (water) — [YEANG, 2009]:**
> "Parallel to the ecological infrastructure is the water infrastructure (the blue infrastructure)
> where the water cycle should be managed to close the loop, although not always possible in locations
> with low rainfall. Rainfall needs to be harvested and recycled. The surface water from rain needs to
> be retained within the site and to be returned to the land for the recharging of groundwater by
> means of filtration beds, pervious roadways and built surfaces, retention ponds and bioswales. Water
> used in the built system needs to be recovered and reused inasmuch as possible."

> "Ecodesign must create sustainable urban drainage systems that can function as wetland habitats.
> This is not only to alleviate flooding, but also to create buffer strips for habitat creation."

> "Waterways should not be culverted … but should be replaced with the introduction of wetlands and
> buffer strips of ecologically functional meadow and woodland habitat."

*(The clause "although not always possible in locations with low rainfall" is directly relevant to a
Riyadh application — he himself concedes the blue infrastructure loop cannot always be closed in arid
climates.)*

**RED (human) — [YEANG, 2009]:**
> "The human infrastructure is the human community, its built environment (buildings, houses etc),
> hardscapes and regulatory systems (laws, regulations, ethics etc). These need to be designed to be
> ecomimetic as human-made ecosystems."

*(Important: "red" is NOT merely "people". It explicitly includes hardscape, law, regulation and
ethics. It is the human-institutional layer.)*

### A.4 BIOINTEGRATION — the three strands, verified

**[YEANG, 2009]** the core proposition:
> "The second design strategy is to regard green design as the seamless and benign environmental
> biointegration of the artificial (the human made) with the natural environment. It is the failure to
> successfully integrate that is the cause of environmental problems."

**[YEANG, 2009]** the strongest formulation of his central claim:
> "In effect if we are able to integrate our business processes, our designs and everything we do or
> make in our built environment (which by definition consists of our buildings, facilities,
> infrastructure, products, refrigerators, toys, etc.) with the natural environment in a seamless and
> benign way, there will be in principle, no environmental problems whatsoever. Successfully achieving
> this is of course easier said than done, but herein lies our challenge."

**THE PROSTHESIS ANALOGY — [YEANG, 2009]:**
> "We can draw an analogy between ecodesign and prosthetics design in surgery. A medical prosthetic
> device has to integrate with its organic host being - the human body. Failure to integrate well
> results in dislocation in both. By analogy, this is what ecodesign in our built environment and in
> our businesses should achieve: a total physical, systemic and temporal integration of our human-made,
> built environment with our organic host in a benign and positive way. Ecodesign is essentially design
> that integrates our artificial systems both mechanically and organically, with its host system being
> the ecosystems."

**THE TAXONOMY, verbatim and confirmed — [YEANG, 2009]:**
> "Designing for bio-integration can be regarded at three aspects: physically, systemically and
> temporally."

*(TAXONOMY VERIFIED. It is three, and the three are physical, systemic, temporal. He writes it as
"three aspects", not "three strands" — "strands" is his word for the four ecoinfrastructures. Use
"aspects" or "levels" for biointegration to be accurate.)*

**1. PHYSICAL integration — [YEANG, 2009]:**
> "Physical and systemic integration requires a discernment of the ecology of the site. Any activity
> from our design or our business takes place with the objective to physically integrate benignly with
> the ecosystems. We must first understand the locality's ecosystem before imposing any human activity
> upon it. Every site has an ecology with a limiting capacity to withstand stresses imposed upon it,
> which if stressed beyond this capacity, becomes irrevocably damaged."

> "We need to ascertain its ecosystem's structure and energy flow, its species diversity and other
> ecological properties and processes. Then we must identify which parts of the site (if any) have
> different types of structures and activities, and which parts are particularly sensitive. Finally, we
> must consider the likely impacts of the intended construction and use."

**His critique of his own inherited method (sieve-mapping / McHarg) — [YEANG, 2009]:**
> "This is, of course, a major undertaking. It needs to be done diurnally over the year and in some
> instances over years. To reduce this lengthy effort, landscape architects developed the sieve-mapping
> technique for landscaping mapping. We must be aware that method generally treats the site's ecosystem
> statically and may ignore the dynamic forces taking place between the layers and within an ecosystem.
> Between each of these layers are complex interactions."

*(This is significant: sieve-mapping / layer-overlay is Ian McHarg's method. Yeang adopts the
inheritance but explicitly criticises it as STATIC. See section on McHarg below.)*

**2. SYSTEMIC integration — [YEANG, 2009]:**
> "Another major design issue is the systemic integration of our built forms and its operational
> systems and internal processes with the ecosystems in nature. This integration is crucial because if
> our built systems and processes do not integrate with the natural systems in nature, then they will
> remain disparate, artificial items and potential pollutants. Their eventual integration after their
> manufacture and use is only through biodegradation. Often, this requires a long-term natural process
> of decomposition."

**3. TEMPORAL integration — [YEANG, 2009]:**
> "Temporal integration involves the conservation of both renewable and non-renewable resources to
> ensure that these are sustainable for future generations. This includes designing for low energy
> built systems that are less or are not dependant on the use of non-renewable energy resources."

### A.5 ECOMIMESIS — verified definition in his own words

**[YEANG, 2009]:**
> "The third design strategy is to regard green design as 'ecomimesis' as imitating ecosystems based on
> itrs [sic] processes, structure, features and functions. This is one of the fundamental premise for
> ecodesign. Our built environment must imitate ecosystems in all respects e.g. recycling, using energy
> from the sun through photosynthesis, systems that heads towards increasing energy efficiency, holistic
> balance of biotic and abiotic constituents in the ecosystem, etc."

*(So the four imitated attributes, in his 2009 wording, are: PROCESSES, STRUCTURE, FEATURES and
FUNCTIONS. Elsewhere he says "properties, structure and processes". Both formulations exist; the
constant trio is processes + structure + one of properties/features, plus functions.)*

**[YEANG, 2009]** — the zero-waste argument, and the "stasis" idea:
> "Nature without humans exists in stasis. Can our businesses and our built environment imitate
> nature's processes, structure, and functions, particularly its ecosystems? For instance, ecosystems
> have no waste. Everything is recycled within. Thus by imitating this, our built environment will
> produce no waste."

> "Ecosystems in a biosphere are definable units containing both biotic and abiotic constituents acting
> together as a whole. From this concept, our businesses and built environment should be designed
> analogously to the ecosystem's physical content, composition and processes. For instance, besides
> regarding our architecture as just art objects or as serviced enclosures, we should regard it as
> artefacts that need to be operationally and eventually integrated with nature."

**THE BIOTIC/ABIOTIC ARGUMENT — this is the intellectual core of his biodiversity turn.
[YEANG, 2009]:**
> "As is self-evident, the material composition of our built environment is almost entirely inorganic,
> whereas ecosystems contain a complement of both biotic and abiotic constituents, or of inorganic and
> organic components."

> "Our myriad of construction, manufacturing and other activities are, in effect, making the biosphere
> more and more inorganic, artificial and increasingly biologically simplified. To continue without
> balancing the biotic content means simply adding to the biosphere's artificiality, thereby making it
> increasingly more and more inorganic. This results in the biological simplification of the biosphere
> and the reduction of its complexity and diversity. We must first reverse this trend and balance our
> built environment with greater levels of biomass, ameliorating biodiversity and ecological
> connectivity in the built forms."

*(**[SYNTHESIS]** This paragraph is the key to reading Yeang correctly. His diagnosis of the
environmental crisis is NOT primarily an energy/carbon diagnosis. It is that human building is
progressively making the planet INORGANIC and biologically simplified. Therefore his prescription is
to add biomass and biodiversity back into built form. This explains both his strength — a genuinely
different problem statement from the carbon orthodoxy — and his blind spot, which is that this
framework has no natural place to put an embodied-carbon number. See section 16.)*

**Materials / cradle-to-cradle — [YEANG, 2009]:**
> "Ecodesign also requires the designer to use green materials and assemblies of materials, and
> components that facilitate reuse, recycling and reintegration for temporal integration with the
> ecological systems. We need to be ecomimetic in our use of materials in the built environment. In
> ecosystems, all living organisms feed on continual flows of matter and energy from their environment
> to stay alive, and all living organisms continually produce wastes. Here, an ecosystem generates no
> waste, one species' waste being another species' food. Thus matter cycles continually through the web
> of life. It is this closing of the loop in reuse and recycling that our human-made environment must
> imitate."

### A.6 ECOLOGICAL NEXUS, CORRIDORS AND CONNECTIVITY — verified

**[YEANG, 2009]** — restoration, not just mitigation:
> "Fourthly, ecodesign can be regarded not only as the creating of new artificial 'living' urban
> ecosystems or rehabilitating existing built environments and cities, but also as one of restoring
> existent impaired and devastated ecosystems regionally within the wider landscape to our designed
> system."

**[YEANG, 2009]** — the term "ecological nexus" used in his own text:
> "Achieving these linkages ensures a wider level of species connectivity, interaction, mobility and
> sharing of resources across boundaries. Such real improvements in ecological nexus enhance
> biodiversity and further increase habitat resilience and species survival. Providing ecological
> corridors and linkages in regional planning is crucial in making urban patterns more biologically
> viable."

**[YEANG, 2009]** — the human-made ecosystem:
> "We must biologically integrate the inorganic aspects and processes of our built environment with the
> landscape so that they mutually become ecosystemic. We must create 'human-made ecosystems' compatible
> with the ecosystems in nature. By doing so, we enhance human-made ecosystems' abilities to sustain
> life in the biosphere."

**HORIZONTAL + VERTICAL CONNECTIVITY — this is the bridge from ecomasterplanning to the tall building.
[YEANG, 2009]:**
> "This new connectivity of the landscape with the built form is both a horizontal and a vertical
> endeavour. An obvious demonstration of horizontal connectivity is the provision of ecological
> corridors and links in regional and local planning that are crucial for making urban patterns more
> biologically viable. Connectivity over impervious surfaces and roads can be achieved by using
> ecological bridges, undercrofts and ramps. Besides improved horizontal connectivity, vertical
> connectivity with human buildings is also necessary since most buildings are not single storey but
> multistorey. Design must extend the ecological corridors vertically upwards, with greenery spanning a
> building from the foundations to the green gardens on the rooftops."

*(**[SYNTHESIS]** This is the single most important sentence for a tower project. It is the theoretical
justification for continuous, unbroken planting from ground to roof — the planting is not decoration
and not "green walls", it is an ECOLOGICAL CORRIDOR turned through 90 degrees. It also implies the
corridor must be CONTINUOUS: a corridor with gaps is not a corridor. This is why isolated green
buildings fail in his terms.)*

**[YEANG, 2009]** — on the green infrastructure as habitat network:
> "These linear wildlife corridors connect existing green spaces and larger green areas, and can create
> new larger habitats in their own right, or may be in the form of newly linked existing woodland belts
> or wetlands, or existing landscape features, such as overgrown railway lines, hedges and waterways.
> Any new green infrastructure must clearly also complement and enhance the natural functions of what
> is already there in the landscape."

> "It reverses the fragmentation of natural habitats and encourages increases in biodiversity to restore
> functioning ecosystems while providing the fabric for sustainable living, and safeguarding and
> enhancing natural features."

### A.7 The fifth strategy — biosphere-scale monitoring

**[YEANG, 2009]:**
> "The fifth strategy for ecodesign is to regard our designed system in the context of the biosphere
> globally as a series of interdependent interactions, whose monitoring is necessary to ensure global
> environmental stasis and repairing of environmental devastations by humans, of natural disasters and
> impacts of our human our built environment, activities and industries. These sets of environmental
> interactions need to be monitored for appropriate corrective action to be immediately taken to
> maintain global ecological stability."

### A.8 The critique of rating tools, and his admission of incompleteness

**[YEANG, 2009]** — the LEED/BREEAM critique, full:
> "Green design has to goes [sic] beyond the conventional rating systems such as LEED or BREEAM, etc.
> which are indeed useful indexes for comparing the greenness of building designs. They are however not
> effective design tools. They are not comprehensive enough in approaching the issues of environmental
> design at the local, regional and global levels."

**[YEANG, 2009]** — a remarkably candid closing admission, and a very quotable one:
> "Generally stated, ecological design is still very much in its infancy. The totally green building or
> green city does not yet exist. There is much more theoretical work, technical research and invention,
> and environmental studies that need to be done and tested before we can have a truly green built
> environment. We all need to continue this great pursuit."

## SOURCE B — "The Ken Yeang guide to green design", The Fifth Estate, 6 May 2009

An essay **written by Yeang himself** (signed "Ken Yeang © 2008") and republished by the Australian
outlet The Fifth Estate on 6 May 2009. The footnote states: "The above essay is expanded in the new
book authored by Dr. Ken Yeang, *Ecodesign: A Manual for Ecological Design*, published by John Wiley
& Sons (2006)."

- URL: https://thefifthestate.com.au/articles/on-green-design/
- Site byline is "by L Blundell, 6 May 2009", but the essay body is signed and copyrighted by Yeang.
  Treat the body as **[YEANG]**, dated 2008.

**This source is authoritative for the design MODES, and it corrects a common error.**

### B.1 THE MODES — there are FIVE, not four. VERIFIED.

**[YEANG, © 2008]** verbatim:
> "In addressing this, we need to look into ways to improve internal comfort conditions. There are
> essentially five modes: Passive Mode or bioclimatic design, Mixed Mode, Full Mode, Productive Mode
> and Composite Mode."

**[YEANG, © 2008]** the hierarchy and its order, verbatim:
> "Designing means looking at Passive Mode strategies first, then Mixed Mode to Full Mode, Productive
> Mode and to Composite Mode, all the while adopting progressive strategies to improve comfort
> conditions relative to external conditions."

> ⚠️ **CORRECTION TO THE BRIEF.** The commonly repeated list of FOUR modes (passive / mixed / full /
> productive) is incomplete. In Yeang's own essay the set is **FIVE**, with **Composite Mode** as the
> fifth and final term. The order he states is: **Passive → Mixed → Full → Productive → Composite.**
> If we print four, we are printing a secondary-source simplification. Recommend printing five, or
> printing "passive mode first" as the principle without enumerating.

**Definitions, all verbatim [YEANG, © 2008]:**

**PASSIVE MODE**
> "Passive Mode is designing for improved comfort conditions over external conditions without the use
> of any electro-mechanical systems. Examples of Passive Mode strategies include adopting appropriate
> building configurations and orientation in relation to the locality's climate, appropriate façade
> design solid-to-glazed area ratio and suitable thermal insulation levels, use of natural ventilation
> and use of vegetation."

**THE PASSIVE-MODE-FIRST RULE — the key postulate, verbatim:**
> "The design strategy for the built form must start with Passive Mode or bioclimatic design. This can
> significantly influence the configuration of the built form and its enclosural form. Therefore, this
> must be the first level of design consideration in the process, following which we can adopt other
> modes to further enhance the energy efficiency."

> "Passive Mode requires an understanding of the climatic conditions of the locality, then designing
> not just to synchronize the built form's design with the local meteorological conditions, but to
> optimize the ambient energy of the locality into a building design with improved internal comfort
> conditions without the use of any electro-mechanical systems."

*(Note the distinction: not merely "synchronise with" the climate but "optimize the ambient energy of
the locality". Passive mode is framed as harvesting, not just avoiding.)*

**MIXED MODE**
> "Mixed Mode is where we use some electro-mechanical (M&E) systems. Examples including ceiling fans,
> double facades, flue atriums and evaporative cooling."

**FULL MODE**
> "Full Mode is the full use of electro-mechanical systems, as in any conventional building, to improve
> the internal conditions of comfort. This often requires use of external energy sources (whether from
> fossil-fuel derived sources or from local ambient sources."  [sic — unclosed bracket in original]

> "If our users insist on having consistent comfort conditions throughout the year, the designed system
> heads towards a Full Mode design."

**PRODUCTIVE MODE**
> "Productive Mode is where the built system generates its own energy (e.g. solar energy using
> photovoltaics, or wind energy)."

**COMPOSITE MODE**
> "Composite Mode is a composite of all the above modes and is a system that varies over the seasons of
> the year."

### B.2 A CRITICAL CAVEAT HE HIMSELF PLACES ON RENEWABLES

One of the most useful and least-quoted passages in his work, directly relevant to embodied carbon.
**[YEANG, © 2008]** verbatim:

> "In the case of Productive Modes, such solar collectors, photo-voltaics and wind energy, these systems
> require sophisticated technological systems. They subsequently increase the inorganic content of the
> built form, its embodied energy content and its use of material resources, with increased attendant
> impacts on the environment."

> **[SYNTHESIS]** This is the closest Yeang comes to embodied-impact accounting, and note what it is
> and is not. He names "embodied energy content" as a real cost, and uses it as an argument AGAINST
> reflexively bolting on PV. But he counts the cost in his own currency — "increases the inorganic
> content of the built form" — not in kgCO₂e. He identifies the right problem and then measures it with
> an unquantified instrument. See section 16.

**[YEANG, © 2008]** — a notably honest concession about comfort:
> "It must be clear now that low-energy design is essentially a user-driven condition and a lifestyle

*(He explicitly concedes that passive design does NOT deliver Full-Mode comfort. In a Gulf climate this

### C.1 ⚠️ THE FOUR ECOINFRASTRUCTURES — HIS 2022 WORDING IS NOT GREEN/BLUE/GREY/RED

Asked whether his priorities had changed, **[YEANG, 2022]** answered verbatim:

> "No. Ecological design has been consistently our primary focus and design agenda. We believe there
> are four sets of ecological infrastructures that need to be bio-integrated into a designed system:
> nature (the ecosystems and the biogeochemical cycles); human society (its socio-economic-political-
> institutional systems); the built environment (artefacts and technologies) and hydrology (water
> management and regimes). We need to synergistically bring all these systems together into a whole."

**A direct, dated, first-person statement of the four-infrastructure model with NO colour labels.**
Compare with his own 2009 paper (Source A), which DOES use the colour labels explicitly.

**Reconciliation table — what he actually calls them, where:**

| | Yeang 2009 (Source A) | Yeang 2022 (Source C, RIBAJ) | Routledge 2019 ToC (per parallel stream, unverified here) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nature | **"green"** — "the green ecoinfrastructure or nature's own utilities" | "nature (the ecosystems and the biogeochemical cycles)" | "nature-based infrastructure" / "Earth's life support system" |
| Water | **"blue"** — "water management and closing of the water cycle by design with sustainable drainage" | "hydrology (water management and regimes)" | "hydrological infrastructure" |
| Engineering | **"grey"** — "the engineering infrastructure being the eco sustainable engineering systems and utilities" | "the built environment (artefacts and technologies)" | "technological infrastructure" |

**FINDINGS ON THE COLOUR LABELS — read before printing anything:**

1. The colour shorthand **IS genuinely his** — not a secondary-source invention. He uses "grey",
   "blue", "green", "red" in quotation marks in his own 2009 paper, with definitions, and heads four
   sections "The Green Infrastructure", "The Grey Infrastructure", "The Blue Infrastructure", "The Red
   (or Human) Infrastructure". **Source A is decisive on this.**
2. **BUT the colour labels belong to the 2006–2009 ECOMASTERPLANNING period.** By 2019 (book chapter
   titles) and 2022 (interview) he uses descriptive names instead: nature-based / hydrological /
   technological / anthropocentric.
3. **There is a real semantic drift in GREY and RED, not just a relabelling.** In 2009, "red"
   explicitly INCLUDED "our human built systems, spaces, hardscapes" alongside society and regulation,
   while "grey" was narrowly the utilities (roads, drains, sewerage, telecoms, power). In 2022, "the
   built environment (artefacts and technologies)" has moved OUT of the human category INTO the
   technological one, leaving "human society" as purely socio-economic-political-institutional.
   **Do not print the colour table alongside the 2022 definitions — they do not align cleanly.**
4. **RECOMMENDATION:** attribute the colour version to *EcoMasterplanning* (Wiley, 2009) / "Strategies
   for Green Design" (2009), and the descriptive version to *Saving the Planet by Design* (Routledge,
   2019) and his 2022 statements. Do not present the colours as his current or only terminology. The
   safest single printable line is his 2022 sentence: first-person, dated, unambiguous.

### C.2 THE INTELLECTUAL LINEAGE — named by him, first person

**[YEANG, 2022]** verbatim:
> "There are a few: Professor Ian McHarg, the landscape architect and planner who invented the
> ecological land use planning technique; the philosopher Alfred North Whitehead who advanced the
> philosophy of the organism; and Ludwig von Bertalanffy, a systems scientist who developed the general
> systems theory."

*(**[SYNTHESIS]** This one sentence explains the whole framework. McHarg gives him the METHOD (layered
ecological land-use analysis — which he adopts then criticises as static, see A.4/B.3). Von Bertalanffy
gives him the ONTOLOGY: a building is a system with inputs, outputs and exchanges across a boundary —
exactly how he defines ecodesign. Whitehead's "philosophy of the organism" gives him the METAPHYSICS:
process and organism as primary rather than static objects, which is why his critique of McHarg is
precisely that McHarg is not processual enough. His theory is McHarg's method rebuilt on Whitehead's
process ontology using von Bertalanffy's systems language.)*

### C.3 ECOMIMESIS — his own 2022 one-line definition

**[YEANG, 2022]** verbatim:
> "No specific project, but before I start pushing up daisies, my dream is to achieve as much as I can
> in my ecological agenda of ecomimesis (the emulating, replicating and augmenting of ecosystem
> attributes) to remake our built environment into constructed (human-made) ecosystems. I'm also
> interested in cybernetic building – applying smart systems to ecological design."

**The cleanest, most quotable definition of ecomimesis found anywhere, in his own words, dated:**
*"the emulating, replicating and augmenting of ecosystem attributes"*.

Note the THREE verbs, and that the third — **augmenting** — is the strong claim: he does not only copy

### C.4 THE ORIGIN OF HIS AGENDA — the 1970s turn, in his own words

**[YEANG, 2022]** verbatim, on moving from Buckminster-Fuller-style autonomous-house engineering to
ecology:
> "One night I was introduced to John Frazer, who was doing research on the 'autonomous house' project,
> an idea first mooted by Buckminster Fuller. He asked me if I'd work on the project there and then,
> and I agreed."

> "However, six months into the project I realised that what we were doing was essentially engineering
> without adequate engineering support from industry. I felt that the bigger picture of ecological
> design needed to be first addressed. So I obtained leave to be a research student; to do a doctorate
> on ecological design and planning, and attended lectures on ecology at the Department of Environmental
> Biology. Ecological design and the sub-set of bioclimatic design became my life's agenda."

**The single most important structural sentence in the whole stream:**
*"Ecological design and the sub-set of bioclimatic design became my life's agenda."*

It establishes the hierarchy of his own terms from his own mouth: **BIOCLIMATIC DESIGN IS A SUBSET OF
ECOLOGICAL DESIGN.** The tower work is the subset; the ecology is the whole. Any presentation that
treats "bioclimatic" as the headline gets his structure backwards.

It also establishes that the ecological turn was a REJECTION of pure eco-engineering — the same
argument he still makes in 2009 ("Green design is not just eco-engineering") and 2022.

### D.1 ★ THE FOURTEEN PRINCIPLES OF ECOLOGICAL DESIGN (2026)

Section 3 is introduced as: *"The general principles, goals and design tactics that define ecological
design as the framework for creating built systems that work in synergy with nature as Constructed
Ecosystem."* Each principle has three parts: **Principle**, **Goal**, **Design**. Titles verbatim:

1. **Ecological design harmonizes built system with nature**
2. **Ecological design creates built environments as living systems**
3. **Ecological design is based on the science-of-ecology as Applied Ecology**
4. **Ecological design ensures no harm to nature, repairs past impacts and regenerates nature**
5. **Ecological design protects species biodiversity and natural processes**
6. **Ecological design remakes and reinvents our built environment (our urban structures and cities)
   as constructed ecosystems**
7. **Ecological design biointegrates our built environment with nature at all scales**
8. **Ecological design responds to the climate of its locality as bioclimatic design**
9. **Ecological design optimises resource efficiency and circularity**
10. **Ecological design prevents pollution of the natural environment**
11. **Ecological design innovates for sustainability**
12. **Ecological design enhances human health, happiness, comfort & well-being**
13. **Ecological design inspires society's stewardship of nature**
14. **Ecological design makes nature visible in its designed systems**

*(**[SYNTHESIS]** Note the shape of this list. Principles 1–7 are ontological — what the built
environment IS. Principle 8 (bioclimatic) is ONE item of fourteen and is subordinate. Principles 9–10
are resource/pollution. Principles 11–14 are human-facing: innovation, HEALTH, ethics, and beauty.
The list ends on aesthetics and visibility, not on performance. This is a designer's manifesto, not an
engineer's.)*

**Selected verbatim text of the principles most relevant to a tower project:**

**Principle 1 — [YEANG-DOC, 2026]:**
> "Principle: Address the current environmental crisis and avert further impairments to the Planet, the
> built environment (its structures, components, systems and processes, production and products),
> together with any impacting human activities must function systemically, physically, benignly and
> seamlessly with nature. Functioning benignly means not negatively disrupting the biosphere's systems,
> its constituents and processes. Seamlessly means being biointegrated without any negative dislocation
> in structure and processes in nature."

*(This is the 2026 definition of the two key adjectives. **"Benign"** = not negatively disrupting.
**"Seamless"** = biointegrated without dislocation. Both terms are now formally defined — earlier
sources used them without definition.)*

> "Goal: To avert the impairments caused by the existing built environment, and to design, regenerate
> and restore any legacy degradation of nature."

> "Design: Design the built environment (its production, operation and disposal over its entire
> life-cycle) to foster ecologically positive and mutually beneficial (symbiotic) relationships with
> nature. The approach being ecology-based is different from what is currently being practiced."

**Principle 2 — the living-system claim [YEANG-DOC, 2026]:**
> "Principle: The built environment must be nature-like and be designed, constructed and operated as a
> living system and not as inert objects, harmful technological systems and resource-depleting
> structures."

> "Design: Design to integrate self-regulating, adaptive and responsive systems that draw from natural
> living processes, so that built systems' inputs, outputs, internal processes and external interactions
> function as active living participants in the Planet's ecological cycles."

**Principle 5 — ★ THE BIODIVERSITY & CONNECTIVITY PRINCIPLE. The most directly usable text in the
whole stream for a tower with continuous planting [YEANG-DOC, 2026]:**
> "Principle: Ecological design safeguards and enhances the Planet's biodiversity in its biomes and
> ecosystems locally, regionally and globally."

> "Goal: Preserve the integrity and resilience of ecosystems, their biodiversity and processes at all
> scales." [line duplicated in the original, sic]

> "Design: Design to conserve, restore and enhance biodiversity and ecosystems processes at all scales
> by embedding and biointegrating habitats within human-made systems and built environments to create
> the built environment as constructed ecosystems. Design must avert fragmentation of ecosystems, retain
> existing ecological continuity and create new designed linkages, such as by connecting ecological
> patches and corridors (both vertically and horizontally in builtforms) and by nature-based design
> solutions that support thriving species, their movements across landscapes and ecosystems. At the same
> time, design must avert disrupting the ecosystems processes, such as photosynthesis processes, the
> production of nutrients, the hydrology of the ecosystem, the microbial life such in the soils, the
> disrupting of species interactions and other ecosystem functions and processes."

*(**"patches and corridors"** is proper landscape-ecology vocabulary (Forman & Godron). Note again
**"both vertically and horizontally in builtforms"** — the 2009 vertical-corridor argument survives
intact into 2026, now with the explicit anti-fragmentation rule: **"Design must avert fragmentation
of ecosystems, retain existing ecological continuity"**. THIS is the theoretical statement of why an
isolated green building fails: a fragment is not a corridor.)*

**Principle 7 — the scale nesting [YEANG-DOC, 2026]:**
> "Design: Design to employ nested, hierarchical, and networked design strategies that connect from the
> micro-scale (materials, components) to the meso-scale (buildings, communities), to the macro-scale
> (regions and biomes) and to the mega scale (biosphere) as a continuous set of relationships system
> inasmuch as achievable."

*(A clean four-scale ladder: **micro (materials/components) → meso (buildings/communities) → macro
(regions/biomes) → mega (biosphere)**. Very presentable.)*

**Principle 8 — ★ BIOCLIMATIC DESIGN, and the passive-first rule restated in 2026 [YEANG-DOC, 2026]:**
> "Principle: The built environment must be first designed as a passive low energy system that
> harmonizes with the climate and microclimate of its locality, minimizing and eliminating fossil-fuel
> energy demand while maximizing comfort through natural means."

> "Goal: To reduce & eliminate dependence on fossil-fuel energy by bioclimate design for enhancing
> human comfort & natural responsiveness prior to using mechanical systems."

> "Design: Design to employ passive design strategies (built form orientation, natural ventilation,
> solar shading, thermal buffering, natural daylighting), integration with landscape elements to create
> built environments that adapt to their specific climatic and seasonal context."

*(**The passive-mode-first hierarchy is CONFIRMED as still doctrine in 2026** — "must be first designed
as a passive low energy system", "prior to using mechanical systems". The five passive strategies named
in 2026 are: **built form orientation, natural ventilation, solar shading, thermal buffering, natural
daylighting**, plus landscape integration. Note **"thermal buffering"** — this is the theoretical home
of the service-core-as-solar-buffer idea.)*

**Principle 9 — circularity / cradle-to-cradle [YEANG-DOC, 2026]:**
> "Principle: Use natural resources prudently without wastage and prevent depletion of 'non-renewable'
> (by human time scales) resources and ensure that the biosphere's cycles and processes are not
> disrupted by wastes, emissions or human activities."

> "Design: Design to prioritize renewable inputs with efficiency, to eliminate waste outputs. To adopt
> circular material use systems as processes of use and reuse to conserve both physical and biological
> resources, to optimise material and energy consumption, to avoid wastes, to avoid depletion of not
> easily renewable resources, to design and make built systems that facilitate reuse and endless
> recycling before their benign reintegration into the biosphere at the end of useful life (as against
> passive accumulation on the planet)."

*(**"endless recycling before their benign reintegration into the biosphere at the end of useful life"**
— cradle-to-cradle and end-of-life, verified, 2026. And the memorable contrast: circularity **"as
against passive accumulation on the planet"**.)*

**Principle 12 — ★★ HEALTH AND WELL-BEING AS A FIRST-ORDER PRINCIPLE. This is the salutogenic thread,
inside his own numbered framework [YEANG-DOC, 2026]:**
> "Principle: Ecological design and action must not be detrimental to human health and must enhancing
> [sic] happiness and well-being, while improving living conditions that support a higher quality of
> life."

> "Goal: Design to ensure and promote human health, happiness, well-being and comfort."

> "Design: Design to incorporate biophilic design solutions to create healthy environments that support
> physical and mental well-being of human society through its physical and eco-psychological (biophilic)
> effects."

*(**THIS SETTLES THE HEALTH QUESTION.** Human health is NOT a by-product in his mature framework — it
is principle 12 of 14, with its own Principle/Goal/Design triad, and the named mechanism is
**biophilic design** acting through **"physical and eco-psychological (biophilic) effects"**, on
**"physical and mental well-being"**. See section 15.)*

**Principle 14 — the aesthetics of visible ecology [YEANG-DOC, 2026]:**
> "Design: Design to make the outcomes of ecological design be visually evident in the built systems by
> translating natural processes, ecological services and ecosystem functions into tangible, evidently
> perceivable aspects of built elements through the designed forms and components so that their
> aesthetics, presence, and functioning are directly experienced and appreciated by human society."

*(A genuine design brief: the ecology must be **visible**, not buried in plant rooms. Useful
justification for expressed planting, expressed water, expressed shading.)*

### D.3 ECOMIMICRY vs BIOMIMICRY — his 2026 formulation

Section 5 asks: *"What does it mean to design through biomimicry, and how does the concept of an
'ecosystem' serve as the blueprint for creating constructed environments?"* Answer, **[YEANG-DOC,
2026]** verbatim:

> "Biomimicry in design involves closely imitating the physical and systemic aspects of biological
> systems to create new systems beneficial to humans and benign to the planet. The process identifies a
> biological system to be mimicked, with the 'ecosystem' being the chosen model for ecological design.
> An ecosystem is an abstract concept from Systems Ecology, representing a community of organisms and
> their physical environment interacting. Its living and non-living components are interconnected
> through nutrient cycles and energy flows, maintaining a balanced state. The objective of ecomimicry
> design is to transform the built environment into 'constructed ecosystems'. This involves emulating,
> replicating, augmenting, and enhancing ecosystem properties, including the provision of 'ecosystem
> services'--nature's ecologically beneficial functions. Applying biomimicry of ecosystem attributes to
> all human-made systems (structures, infrastructures, artifacts, and technological systems) is the
> fundamental basis and comprehensive goal of ecological design."

> ⚠️ **THE DISTINCTION, PRECISELY STATED — AND IT IS NOT THE ONE PEOPLE ASSUME.**
> Yeang does **NOT** set ecomimesis *against* biomimicry as a rival. He treats biomimicry as the
> **general method** ("imitating … biological systems") and then makes a **choice of scale**: the
> biological system he elects to mimic is **the ecosystem**, not the organism. Ecomimicry is therefore
> **biomimicry performed at the ecosystem level.** In his own words: "the 'ecosystem' being the chosen
> model", and "biomimicry of ecosystem attributes".
>
> **So the correct one-line framing is:** biomimicry conventionally copies an ORGANISM's form or
> mechanism (a leaf, a termite mound, a shark's skin); Yeang's ecomimicry copies an ECOSYSTEM's
> structure, processes, functions and services. He also closes the document by saying ecological
> design is "rooted in the biomimicry of ecosystems", which is the same point.
>
> **DO NOT print "Yeang rejects biomimicry."** That is not supported. What is supported is that he
> regards organism-level mimicry as the wrong unit of imitation for the environmental crisis, because
> only the ecosystem has the closed loops, the biotic/abiotic balance and the services.
>
> **NOTE ON SPELLING:** this 2026 document uses **"ecomimicry"**. His 2019 Routledge book title and his
> 2009/2022 statements use **"ecomimesis"**. Both are his. Some sources use them interchangeably; the
> 2019 book's own subtitle is "Reinventing Our World Through Ecomimesis" while its Google Books/Amazon
> records carry "Ecomimicry" strings. Treat them as **the same concept under two spellings** and pick
> one for the deck. **UNVERIFIED:** whether he has ever formally distinguished the two words.

**Also in section 5, the "nature-to-nature" reframing [YEANG-DOC, 2026]:**
> "Ecological design is a nature-based approach that views the built environment as applied ecology,
> aiming to avert degradation and regenerate naatural [sic] systems. It transforms human-made elements
> into 'nature-like, ecology-based systems'. The current 'technology-to-nature' interface, which causes
> environmental crisis, is replaced by a seamless 'nature-to-nature' biointegration where the built
> environment functions harmoniously with natural systems. … The goal is to 're-nature and re-wild'
> urban environments into ecologically balanced systems, prioritizing nature over technological or
> anthropocentric approaches. Ultimately, ecological design is rooted in the biomimicry of ecosystems."

*(**"technology-to-nature" replaced by "nature-to-nature"** is an excellent, compact, printable idea.
And "re-nature and re-wild" is his 2026 verb pair. Note also "prioritizing nature over technological
or anthropocentric approaches" — the hierarchy among the infrastructures, restated.)*

**The prosthesis analogy survives to 2026** — the section-5 diagram is captioned with the pairing

### D.6 ★ THE 2026 CRITIQUE OF GREEN RATING SYSTEMS — the clearest statement he has made

Section 11 poses the question: *"In what critical ways does ecological design diverge from conventional
green building certification systems, and what implications does this have for achieving true planetary
health?"* Answer, **[YEANG-DOC, 2026]** verbatim and in full:

> "Ecological design fundamentally differs from prescriptive green accreditation systems (like LEED,
> BREEAM, CASBI). These systems often prioritize technological engineering aspects, such as net-zero
> energy and water management or decarbonization, making ecological considerations secondary. For
> example, the 'Passivhaus' approach focuses on energy efficiency but may not adequately consider local
> ecology. In contrast, ecological design is performance-based, not prescriptive. While it incorporates
> benign technological aspects like energy and water systems, these are always secondary to ecology.
> Bioclimatic design, which responds to local climate for low-energy systems, is considered a subset of
> ecological design, as ecological design extends beyond climate to consider the broader natural
> environment and its ecology."

> **[SYNTHESIS] — THIS IS THE MOST IMPORTANT PARAGRAPH IN THE WHOLE STREAM FOR OUR PURPOSES.** It does
> four things at once:
> 1. **Names the targets:** LEED, BREEAM, CASBI ("CASBEE" is presumably meant), and Passivhaus.
> 2. **States the charge precisely:** not that they are wrong, but that they are **prescriptive**
>    rather than **performance-based**, and that they **make ecology secondary** by foregrounding
>    engineering.
> 3. **Says the quiet part out loud:** *"decarbonization"* is explicitly listed among the things these
>    systems over-prioritise. **He is, in 2026, positioning decarbonisation as a subordinate concern to
>    ecology.** This is the single most quotable evidence for the critique stream's thesis. It is not
>    that he ignores carbon — it is that he **actively ranks it below ecology**.
> 4. **Restates the hierarchy for the third time:** *"Bioclimatic design … is considered a subset of
>    ecological design."* This now has three independent confirmations (C.4 2022, D.6 2026, and the
>    logic of principle 8 of 14).

### J.2 ★ HOW HE CONNECTS ECOLOGY TO HEALTH — the actual mechanism

The chapter's own framing of the two authors: **"Ken Yeang, who is the founder of ecological design,
and Alan Dilani, the founder of salutogenic design principles"** — note the extremely strong (and
self-applied) claim "the founder of ecological design".

**★ THE LOAD-BEARING SENTENCE. The link is INFRASTRUCTURAL, not psychological. [YEANG & DILANI,
2021]:**
> "The challenge for ecological design is to provide a green context for a healthy society, dealing
> with built infrastructure that creates clean air, clean water, clean food, and clean land."

and, restated later in the chapter:

> "Promoting a healthy lifestyle and healthy spaces depends on ecological design that primarily deals
> with infrastructure by creating clean air, clean water, clean food and clean land."

**★ THE DIVISION OF LABOUR BETWEEN THE TWO DISCIPLINES — the single clearest statement of how the two
halves fit together [YEANG & DILANI, 2021]:**
> "salutogenic design can provide social organisation, structure, and function in society; while
> ecological design can work to continually restore the natural environment."

> **[SYNTHESIS] — THIS IS THE ARCHITECTURE OF THE ARGUMENT, AND IT IS IMPORTANT TO GET RIGHT.**
> Yeang does NOT claim that ecological design directly produces health through a psychological
> mechanism. His claim is a **two-step**:
> 1. **Ecological design produces the physical preconditions of health** — clean air, clean water,
>    clean food, clean land — delivered via "water management and retention, natural heating and
>    cooling, as well as renewable energy".
> 2. **Salutogenic design (Dilani's contribution) then works on the psychosocial layer** — stimulation,
>    coherence, meaning, stress reduction.
> The two are presented as **"intertwined"** and **"compatible"**, not as one derived from the other.
> **So: health enters Yeang's framework through ECOSYSTEM SERVICES, and Dilani supplies the
> psychosocial half that Yeang's own ecology-based theory does not contain.** That is the honest
> reading, and it is a stronger, more defensible story than claiming Yeang always had a health theory.

**Also relevant — a rare admission that buildings are part of the emissions problem
[YEANG & DILANI, 2021]:**
> "Environmental challenges such as climate change and other impairments within natural ecosystems are
> also architectural problems, given the bulk of building emissions and waste in landfills."

### J.3 ★ SALUTOGENESIS EXPLAINED — Antonovsky, and the three-part "sense of coherence"

The chapter grounds salutogenic design in **Aaron Antonovsky**'s work (cited as Antonovsky 1979, 1991).
Key structure, as set out in the chapter:

- Health research divides into **pathogenic** (what causes disease; aim = medical treatment) and
  **salutogenic** (what creates health) perspectives.
- The salutogenic question is, in the chapter's words, **"what creates health?"** rather than what
  causes disease.
- Antonovsky's **"sense of coherence" (SOC)**: "a person with a high sense of coherence chooses the
  most appropriate coping strategy in a stressful situation." "A strong sense of coherence predicts
  good health, and a low sense of coherence predicts poor health."
- **SOC has three components: (1) comprehensibility; (2) manageability; (3) meaningfulness.**

**★ THE THREE SALUTOGENIC DESIGN PRINCIPLES — the architectural translation of Antonovsky's three
components. Verbatim headings and glosses [YEANG & DILANI, 2021]:**

> **(1) "Environmental comprehensibility"** — "requires environmental orderliness, predictability, and
> legibility. This includes, for instance, the importance of creating visual order in the built
> environment with legible, intuitive wayfinding, the elimination of visual chaos, etc."
>
> **(2) "Environmental manageability"** — "requires effective familiarity and social support etc."
>
> **(3) "Environmental meaningfulness"** — "requires the provision of visual and aesthetic meaning,
> interesting positive stimuli, satisfaction, and attendant spaces for contemplation and restoration of
> the mind within the built environment."

**And the function of salutogenic design [YEANG & DILANI, 2021]:**
> "The basic function of salutogenic design is to start a mental process by attracting human attention,
> which may reduce anxiety and promote positive psychological emotions."

*(**[SYNTHESIS]** This is a genuinely usable three-part design test: is the building **legible**, is it
**manageable/socially supportive**, is it **meaningful**? It maps onto wayfinding, social space and
contemplative space respectively — three things a tower can actually be designed for and assessed on.)*

  experiences, and the impact of a healthy lifestyle)."

**★ THE FIVE PSYCHOSOCIAL DESIGN PARAMETERS. The chapter names five architectural parameter families
"closely linked to the perception of positive and negative stress" — this is effectively the content
of the matrix, and it is highly specifiable. Verbatim [YEANG & DILANI, 2021]:**

1. **stimulation** — "(intensity, variety, complexity, mystery, novelty, noise, light, odour, colour,
   crowding, visual exposure, proximity to circulation, adjacencies)"

3. **affordances** — "(ambiguity, sudden perceptual changes, perceptual-cue conflict, feedback)"
4. **control** — "(crowding, boundaries, climatic and light controls, spatial hierarchy, territoriality,

5. **restoration** — "(minimal distraction, stimulus, shelter, fascination, solitude)"

*(**[SYNTHESIS]** This is the most directly operational thing in the entire Yeang corpus for a
human-experience argument. Note **"control"** includes "climatic and light controls" — which is

# 15. HEALTH / SALUTOGENIC DESIGN — CONSOLIDATED ANSWER

**Does human health appear in his theoretical framework? YES — and more substantially than expected.**

Four dated pieces of evidence:

1. **2021 — the Dilani book.** He co-edited it and co-wrote the 42-page lead chapter. Not a foreword.
   He is described in it as "the founder of ecological design" opposite Dilani as "the founder of
   salutogenic design principles". (Source J)
2. **2021 — the "Design Health Matrix"**, explicitly "co-developed by Dilani and Yeang", applied to a
   real building (the Health House, Èze). Joint design-tool authorship, not endorsement. (J.4)
3. **2026 — Principle 12 of 14:** "Ecological design enhances human health, happiness, comfort &
   well-being", with its own Principle/Goal/Design triad and **biophilic design** named as the
   mechanism. Health is inside the numbered doctrine. (D.1)
4. **2022** — "I regard the purpose of architecture as to give happiness and pleasure to the lives of
   the people who use or visit our buildings." (C.7)

**HOW HE CONNECTS ECOLOGY TO HEALTH — the two-step:**
- **Step 1 (his half):** ecological design delivers the *physical* preconditions of health — "clean
  air, clean water, clean food, and clean land" — through infrastructure: water management and
  retention, natural heating and cooling, renewable energy, green space.
- **Step 2 (Dilani's half):** salutogenic design delivers the *psychosocial* half —
  comprehensibility, manageability, meaningfulness — building the occupant's "sense of coherence" and
  so their capacity to cope with stress.
- Their own formula: *"salutogenic design can provide social organisation, structure, and function in
  society; while ecological design can work to continually restore the natural environment."*

**THE HONEST CAVEAT [SYNTHESIS].** Health is **not** native to Yeang's original theory. Nothing in the
2008/2009 primary essays (A, B) concerns human health; those texts are about ecosystems,
biointegration and waste. Health arrives in force in 2021 **through the collaboration with Dilani**,
and is absorbed into his own numbered framework by 2026. **The correct claim: Yeang *fused* ecological
design with salutogenic design, adopting a health framework he did not originate — exactly what
co-editing a book with the founder of the other discipline implies.** Claiming he had a health theory
all along would be false and easily falsified. Claiming he now holds a fused ecological-salutogenic
position is true, dated and documented.

**The strongest bridge between his two halves** is occupant **control** — "climatic and light controls"
appears inside the salutogenic parameter set (J.4) and is simultaneously a passive-mode strategy.
Operable, occupant-controlled environment is both a low-energy device and a health device. That is the
most defensible single place to join the ecology argument to the wellbeing argument.

# 16. ★ CARBON vs BIODIVERSITY — WHERE THE FRAMEWORK HAS NO ANSWER

**The brief asked me not to paper over this. I am not going to. But the picture is more interesting
than "he ignores carbon", and the critique needs correcting in two directions — one sharper, one
softer.**

### 16.1 What is TRUE and well-evidenced

**He explicitly and repeatedly subordinates carbon to ecology.** Not an omission — a stated position,
held consistently for at least twelve years:

- **2014:** "the almost arrogant perception that carbon neutrality and energy efficiency are all there
  is to eco-design… This is at the expense of ecology and biodiversity aspects." (E)
- **2021:** rating and accreditation systems "such as LEED, BREEAM, **carbon profiling**, etc… are not
  ecologically comprehensive." (J.6)
- **2026:** accreditation systems "often prioritize technological engineering aspects, such as net-zero
  energy and water management or **decarbonization**, making ecological considerations secondary…
  these are always **secondary to ecology**." (D.6)

**That last is the smoking gun.** In 2026 he lists *decarbonisation* among the things over-prioritised,
and states technological aspects are "always secondary to ecology". **He is not failing to address
carbon; he is deliberately ranking it below ecology, and saying so.**

**In no source located in this stream does he publish a carbon figure.** No kgCO₂e/m², no LCA boundary,
no EPD, no A1–A5 breakdown, no baseline, no methodology, no third-party verification. The only hard
performance number found anywhere is one operational claim: **Suasana "consumes 30 per cent less energy
than a comparative similar building"** (C.7) — no stated baseline, no metric, no verification.

**★ AND THE SAME IS TRUE OF HIS BIODIVERSITY CLAIMS — this is the deeper finding.** He asserts
"constructed habitats within the built form to enrich the local biodiversity" (2022) with no species
count, no biodiversity net gain percentage, no monitoring data, no post-occupancy ecological survey.
**So the critique is not "he measures ecology instead of carbon" — it is that HE LARGELY MEASURES
NEITHER.** He proposes a richer set of aims and then evidences them qualitatively. That is the accurate
and considerably more forceful version of the criticism.

### 16.2 What must be CORRECTED — carbon IS now in his vocabulary

The claim that "he has never engaged with carbon" is **no longer accurate as of the 2026 material**;
overstating it would be a factual error a well-briefed client could catch:

- **2022:** names "near net zero energy and carbon, near net zero wastes" as current requirements —
  though pointedly as things making projects "more complex and onerous". (C.6)
- **2026 (D.2), verbatim entries in the "built systems" domain of his own model:** **"buildings (zero
  carbon materials)"**, **"low embodied energy & carbon neutral construction operations"**, **"carbon
  capture systems"**, "hydrogen production (without emitting carbon)".
- **2026 energy diagram** carries the labels **"operational energy level"**, **"Embodied Energy"**,
  **"Net Zero"**, **"embodied carbon"**.
- **2026 energy-domain target is literally "NSE&ZC — Net Surplus Energy & Zero Carbon".**

**So the honest statement: embodied carbon appears in his 2026 framework as a named category — but only
ever as a label on a diagram or a bullet in a list. It is never quantified, never given a method, never
given a target value, never applied to a named building.**

**And note the 2008 precedent (B.2):** he engaged with embodied impact early and intelligently, warning
that PV and wind "increase… its embodied energy content and its use of material resources". He counted
it in his own currency — "increase the inorganic content of the built form" — not in kgCO₂e. **He has
had the right instinct about embodied impact since 2008 and has never built an accounting method for
it.**

### 16.3 WHY the framework structurally cannot produce a carbon number [SYNTHESIS]

This is the analytically important part. It is not laziness; it follows from his premises.

His diagnosis (A.5, verbatim) is that human building makes the biosphere **"more and more inorganic,
artificial and increasingly biologically simplified"**. The crisis is a **loss of biotic content and
complexity**. The remedy is therefore to **add biomass, biodiversity and connectivity**.

**Carbon does not fit that ontology.** A tonne of CO₂e is not a biotic or abiotic *constituent* of a
site; it is a global flux. His whole method is **site-, locality- and ecosystem-based** — "we must
first understand the locality's ecosystem before imposing any human activity upon it". A framework
built on the *particular ecology of a place* has no native unit for a *globally fungible* quantity.
That is why carbon enters his work only as an imported vocabulary sitting alongside the model, never as
an output of it.

**The five "net" targets of 2026 make the point precisely:** NPEI (Net Positive Ecological Impact) is
the flagship, and **it has no defined unit**. NSE&ZC and NZW/E — the two that *could* be quantified —
are borrowed engineering targets. **His own signature metric is the unmeasurable one.**

### 16.4 How to handle this — recommendation

1. **Do not claim Yeang solves embodied carbon.** He does not, and the framework is not built to.
2. **Do not repeat "he never mentions carbon"** — as of 2026 that is falsifiable (D.2).
3. **The defensible and genuinely strong position:** Yeang supplies the *ecological* and *health*
   agenda — biodiversity, connectivity, ecosystem services, salutogenics — which carbon accounting does
   not capture and which rating systems demonstrably under-weight. **Carbon accounting must be supplied
   separately and rigorously, by us, alongside him.** His own 2009 words license this: *"ecological
   design is still very much in its infancy… much more theoretical work, technical research and
   invention… need[s] to be done"* (A.8).
4. **If challenged on evidence:** his own answer, on his publisher's page, is that the buildings are
   **prototypes** — Kishnani's review: "his projects, even where they do not reach full potential, are
   prototypes, he says" (G.4). Use his candour rather than defending a claim he never made.

# 19. SUMMARY — THE CORE INTELLECTUAL ARCHITECTURE [SYNTHESIS]

**One sentence.** Yeang holds that the environmental crisis is caused by the built environment failing
to integrate with nature — making the biosphere progressively more inorganic and biologically
simplified — and that the remedy is to design buildings and cities as **constructed ecosystems** by
imitating not organisms but **whole ecosystems**, integrating four infrastructures (nature, water,
technology, human society) so completely that the artificial becomes a benign prosthesis to its living
host.

**The five moves that make up the theory:**

1. **ECOCENTRICITY** — the epistemology. Design governed by the *science of ecology*, taught as
   "applied ecology". Not metaphor, not aesthetics: ecology as the source discipline.
2. **BIOINTEGRATION** — the goal. Seamless and benign integration of artificial with natural, across
   **three aspects: physical, systemic, temporal** (triple-verified 2008/2009/2021). The prosthesis is
   the governing image.
3. **ECOMIMESIS / ECOMIMICRY** — the method. Imitate the *ecosystem concept* — its attributes,
   properties, processes, structure, features and functions — rather than an organism's form. Hence
   zero waste, closed loops, biotic/abiotic balance. **This is biomimicry performed at ecosystem scale,
   not a rejection of biomimicry.**
4. **THE FOUR INFRASTRUCTURES** — the instrument. Green/nature-based, blue/hydrological,
   grey/technological, red/anthropocentric, woven into a **"quatrobrid" constructed ecosystem** — with
   the *nature* infrastructure taking **precedence** over the engineering one.
5. **CONNECTIVITY** — the design consequence, and the one that matters most for a tower. Ecology works
   through **patches and corridors**; fragmentation kills it; therefore ecological corridors must run
   **horizontally AND vertically**, "from the foundations to the green gardens on the rooftops". **An
   isolated green building is, in his terms, not ecological at all.**

**Layered on top, and later:** bioclimatic design as an explicitly *subordinate subset* (passive mode
first, then mixed, full, productive, composite); life-cycle and design-for-disassembly specified at the
level of the joint (mechanical fixings, demountable, modular); and — from 2021 — **salutogenic health**,
fused via Dilani, so the framework now claims both a planet and a person.

**The consistent adversary is the checklist.** LEED, BREEAM, CASBEE, Passivhaus, carbon profiling:
useful, but "not effective design tools", "not ecologically comprehensive", and — the charge that
matters — they make **ecology secondary**.

**And that is also the fault line.** By ranking ecology above decarbonisation, and by evidencing ecology
qualitatively rather than numerically, he has built a framework broader and more humane than the carbon
orthodoxy but harder to audit than it. His own answer is that the buildings are prototypes and that
"ecological design is still very much in its infancy". That is a defensible answer — but it is not a
measurement, and any use of his thinking should supply the measurement he does not.

**And on the tower specifically**, his position is unexpectedly modest and therefore unexpectedly
useful: the skyscraper is "probably the most ecologically unfriendly of all building types", and the
case for building one rests on necessity, density and the absence of a viable alternative — not on the
claim that a tall building is good for the planet. **A proposal that starts from that concession is far
harder to attack than one that starts from a green claim.**
