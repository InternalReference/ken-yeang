## The arc, readable from the titles alone

1. **1986–1992, the regionalist.** *The Tropical Verandah City*, *Tropical Urban Regionalism*, *The Architecture of Malaysia*. Before the green tower, the argument is about tropical Asian identity in architecture.
2. **1994–2002, the skyscraper decade.** *Bioclimatic Skyscrapers*, *Designing With Nature*, *The Green Skyscraper*, *Service Cores*, *Reinventing the Skyscraper*. The famous Yeang.
3. **2006–2010, the systematiser.** *Ecodesign: A Manual*, *EcoMasterplanning*, *Dictionary of Ecodesign*. He stops writing manifestos and starts writing method.
4. **2016–2021, the late synthesis.** *Constructed Ecosystems*, *It's Not Easy Being Green*, *Saving the Planet by Design*, *At One with Nature*.

### Saving the Planet by Design: Reinventing Our World Through Ecomimesis (Routledge, 2019)

**Verified record.** Routledge, 1st edition, published 25 October 2019, copyright 2020, 214 pages, 9 colour illustrations, ISBN 9780415685818. Source: publisher page, routledge.com/Saving-the-Planet-by-Design-Reinventing-Our-World-Through-Ecomimesis/Yeang/p/book/9780415685818

**Verified table of contents** (from the publisher page, reproduced as listed):

> Foreword · Preface · 1. Reinventing the human-made world to address the sustainability equation · 2. Redefining design to include the ecological sciences: The principle of ecocentricity · 3. Reinventing the built environment by 'ecomimicry' · 4. Ecological Design as the biointegration of a set of 'infrastructures': The 'quatrobrid' constructed ecosystem · 5. Nature-based infrastructure – Earth's 'life support system' · 6. Hydrological infrastructure · 7. Technological infrastructure · 8. Anthropocentric infrastructure · 9. Being 'at one' with Nature · Glossary · Bibliography · Index

**What it actually argues.** Two principles carry the book. **Ecocentricity** means design should be governed by the science of ecology rather than by human-centred preference or by compliance scoring. **Ecomimesis** means designing and making the entire built environment, including all artefacts, by emulating and replicating the *ecosystem* concept — not merely copying biological forms.

The structural payoff is chapter 4's **"quatrobrid" constructed ecosystem**: the built environment understood as the biointegration of four infrastructures, which chapters 5 to 8 then take one at a time — **nature-based** (chapter 5, called Earth's life support system), **hydrological** (6), **technological** (7), and **anthropocentric** (8). This is the verified naming. Note that this maps onto, but does *not* use, the green/blue/grey/red shorthand often attributed to him in secondary sources; treat the colour labels as UNVERIFIED against this book.

Its sharpest polemical claim, stated on the publisher page: designing with ecology is "the authentic approach to green design from which the next generation of green design will emerge, **going beyond current use of accreditation systems**." That is a direct shot at LEED/BREEAM-style checklist certification.

**What this book adds.** It is his late synthesis and the clearest single statement of the ecomimesis thesis. It also reframes the ambition from building-scale to planetary: the goal is an "ecotopia" of net-positive socio-environmental consequences.

**Reviews carried by the publisher** (useful, but note these are publisher-selected endorsements, not independent criticism):
- **Mohsen Mostafavi** (Dean, Harvard GSD): calls it "an eloquent argument for the role that design, as a form of 'ecomimesis,' can and should play."
- **Ali Malkawi** (Harvard Center for Green Building and Cities): "This book is timely as it centers the debate on the importance of holistic ecological design."
- **Michael John Wells** (ecologist, Biodiversity by Design): describes Yeang's vision of "hybrid urban ecosystems in which designed abiotic and biotic components interact synergistically."
- One quoted review makes a notably candid concession, describing his projects as prototypes "**even where they do not reach full potential**" — an acknowledgement, inside his own marketing copy, of the gap between the theory and the built results.

**Biographical detail confirmed here:** trained at the Architectural Association School (UK) and Cambridge University; the doctorate at Cambridge in the 1970s was on ecological design and planning.

### Ecodesign: A Manual for Ecological Design (Wiley-Academy, 2006)

**Verified record.** First published London: Wiley-Academy, 2006, ISBN 9780470852910. Wiley paperback/reissue 2008, Hoboken NJ, ISBN 9780470997789, 506 pages (Internet Archive catalogue record: archive.org/details/ecodesignmanualf0000yean). Open Library holds both editions as separate works (/works/OL1941187W and /works/OL19823756W). Google Books lists a 499-page 2006 Wiley-Academy record.

**Verified structure.** Contrary to the assumption that this is a densely subdivided treatise, the book uses a deliberately simple three-part organisation. Per Rand Ekman's review, the book "is broken into three chapters — **General Premises and Strategies**, **Design Instructions**, and **Other Considerations**," each broken into subtopics (for example design for water conservation, recycling, harvesting) that function as independent, self-contained resources. Source: The Fifth Estate, archived at web.archive.org/web/20220519182721/https://thefifthestate.com.au/articles/book-review-ecodesign-a-manual-for-ecological-design/

**What it actually argues.** The stated purpose is to give designers a set of instructions so that what we make becomes an "integral and benign part of life on the planet." Ekman is precise about the register: the book "is not intended to convince, encourage, or cajole the reader" and is "neither about doom and gloom, nor is it about cheerleading." It is a technical manual written, in his phrase, "by a preeminent architectural designer from an ecologist's perspective."

The central move is one of scope. Ecodesign "is not about how to engineer an efficient building, or how to efficiently integrate professional services... rather it is about the fundamental integration of the building and built environment with the natural environment." Efficiency is not the object; biointegration is.

Publisher description (Google Books) adds the material-cycle agenda: low-consumption structures that use non-renewable resources minimally, materials selected for reduced ecological impact, design for disassembly, reuse and recycling, and end-of-life reintegration into natural cycles.

**On the "design matrix".** Yeang is widely credited with a four-part partitioned **interaction matrix** describing exchanges between the designed system and the ecosystem — commonly rendered as external interdependencies (L1), internal interdependencies (L2), inputs from environment to built system (L21), and outputs from built system to environment (L12). This framework is real and recurs across his writing, but I could **not verify the L1/L2/L21/L12 labelling directly against the text of *Ecodesign*** from accessible sources. **Mark UNVERIFIED** pending sight of the book itself; do not quote the quadrant codes in client-facing material without checking a physical copy.

**Critique (Rand Ekman, Director of Environmental Design at OWP/P).** He rates it "a highly valuable resource" and calls the organisation of an "enormously broad and complex topic" admirable. But his criticism is specific and worth carrying: the organisational scheme "sometimes tends to compete with the content," with cross-references to other subtopics obscuring the primary material, and "the book's format and graphic design also tend vie for attention with the content." He concedes this is a criticism of non-content issues but warns readers may not work past the difficulty. In short: the method is sound, the book is hard to read.

**What this book adds.** It is the methodological core of the whole output — the point at which Yeang stops writing manifestos about towers and writes a general procedure for ecological design at any scale.

### Designing With Nature: The Ecological Basis for Architectural Design (McGraw-Hill, 1995)

**Verified record.** McGraw-Hill, 1995, 243 pages, ISBN 0070723176 / 9780070723177. Open Library /works/OL1941185W. Spanish translation *Proyectar Con La Naturaleza*, Editorial Gustavo Gili, 2000, 198pp, ISBN 9788425217630.

**What it actually argues** (from the verified publisher description). This is the book that converts his Cambridge doctorate into professional method. Its pitch is explicitly **anti-piecemeal**: "Unlike other books that offer a piece-meal approach to ecological design, *Designing With Nature* provides you with a comprehensive framework." The claim is to offer "the first truly holistic approach to making architecture compatible with a broad range of vital ecological concerns."

Three moves structure it: understand the impact different kinds of built works have on the natural environment; mitigate damage to a building site and its natural resources; and demonstrate ecologically sound design by example.

Named methods in the publisher copy — useful because they show how early his vocabulary was fixed: **low-energy and bioclimatic design, recycling and disposal of materials, ecological land-use planning, natural resource management**, material selection for long life span and low maintenance, and "low-environmental-impact" buildings.

**What this book adds.** The intellectual foundation stone. The title deliberately echoes Ian McHarg's *Design with Nature* (1969), positioning Yeang as the figure who takes McHarg's regional-planning ecology and drives it down into the design of individual buildings. Everything from *Ecodesign* onward is an elaboration of the framework first set out here.

### The Green Skyscraper: The Basis for Designing Sustainable Intensive Buildings (Prestel, 1999)

**Verified record.** Prestel, 1999, 244 pages, ISBN 3791319930 / 9783791319933. Open Library /works/OL1941202W.

**What it actually argues** (from the verified book jacket). The structure is stated plainly: the book "presents a general framework for looking at ecological design, followed by an examination of its fundamental premises and how these might be applied to the design of **the contemporary large building**."

The specific contribution is isolating *which* aspects of building actually bear on the environment — "the use of energy and materials and their physical impacts on ecosystems" — and then reasoning from those to tall-building design. The subtitle's phrase **"intensive buildings"** is the key term: tall buildings are treated as intensive concentrations of resource use and ecosystem impact, which is precisely why they are the hardest and most consequential case for ecological design.

It is evidence-led rather than purely theoretical: "illustrated by examples from Yeang's experiments, projects and research, and includes references to research work by others."

**What this book adds.** It is the book that made his international reputation and put "green skyscraper" into general use. It moves him from a regional Asian figure to the global reference point on ecological tall buildings.

**Note on the frequently-cited passive/mixed/full-mode energy hierarchy and the skycourt, wind-scoop, vertical-landscaping and service-core-as-solar-buffer devices.** These are genuinely Yeang's and recur across his work, but I could **not verify from accessible sources which specific book first sets them out** — they appear across *The Skyscraper Bioclimatically Considered* (1996), *The Green Skyscraper* (1999), *Service Cores* (2000) and *Constructed Ecosystems* (2016). **Attribution to a specific title: UNVERIFIED.**

### EcoMasterplanning (Wiley, 2009)

**Verified record.** Wiley, 2009, 256 pages, ISBN 0470697296 / 9780470697290. Open Library /works/OL1941189W.

**What it actually argues** (from the verified book jacket). This is the scale-jump from building to territory. It is "a comprehensive introduction to the ideas and principles that lie behind the many masterplans that he has designed," in "a highly visually driven format."

The jacket says the book illustrates **over 30 masterplans** — note that secondary sources including Wiley's own marketing copy elsewhere say "over 20," so the count is inconsistent across sources; the jacket text says 30. Sites named: **Amsterdam, Guangzhou** (jacket spells it "Gwongzhou"), **Istanbul, Kuala Lumpur** (jacket spells it "Kuala Lumpar"), **Kuwait, Macau, Mumbai, Singapore and Vancouver**. The two misspellings are in the source and are reproduced here rather than silently corrected.

The method is legible from the format: each masterplan is "accompanied by explanatory diagrams of the ideas and devices used," so the book functions as a pattern-book of transferable ecomasterplanning devices rather than a portfolio.

**What this book adds.** It establishes that his method is scale-independent — the same ecological reasoning that shapes a tower shapes a district. For a masterplan-adjacent project this is the most directly applicable of his books after *Ecodesign*.

## Flagged finding: the 2021 salutogenic book

***Ecological and Salutogenic Design for a Sustainable Healthy Global Society*** (Cambridge Scholars, 2021), co-edited with **Alan Dilani** of the International Academy for Design and Health.

Salutogenic design is health-generating design: the proposition that a building should actively make occupants well, rather than merely avoid making them ill.

**Verified record.** Cambridge Scholars Publishing, 2021. ISBN 9781527579927 (paperback; an ebook edition also exists). Listed as "By: Ken Yeang, Alan Dilani" on the publisher page — note the publisher credits them as authors, though the content is a multi-contributor volume, so "edited by" is the more accurate description of their actual role. Source: cambridgescholars.com/product/978-1-5275-7992-7

**Verified publisher description**, which carries the argument in compressed form:

> "This volume brings together several leading scientists and practitioners from around the world to discuss the ecological and salutogenic design principles for creating a healthy built environment. These principles and applications are the most important scientific topic of health promotion that provides the context for a healthy lifestyle. The challenge for ecological design is to provide a green context for a healthy society dealing with built infrastructure that creates clean air, clean water, clean food, and clean land, which in turn are necessary for human health and wellbeing. In this book, these principles are intertwined with those of salutogenic design, which support human health globally."

**What it actually argues.** The logical structure is the valuable part, and it is a chain, not a list. Ecological design produces **clean air, clean water, clean food and clean land**; those four are the necessary preconditions of human health and wellbeing; salutogenic design then builds on that base to actively promote health rather than merely protect it. Health is therefore presented as the *output* of ecological infrastructure, not as a parallel or competing agenda.

Note the framing of health promotion as providing "the context for a healthy lifestyle" — the building is positioned as the setting that makes healthy living possible, which is the core salutogenic move (after Aaron Antonovsky, whose salutogenesis concept underpins Dilani's work).

**Co-editor.** Alan Dilani, PhD, founder of the International Academy for Design and Health and of the journal *World Health Design*; he is the figure who has driven salutogenic design internationally.

**Why it matters to this project.** It lands directly on the Burj Fitrah **Holistic** requirement and the health-across-five-systems framing. It means the health argument is not a bolt-on to the green argument. Yeang himself fused the two, in print, in 2021, and the four-clean-outputs chain is a ready-made structure for arguing that environmental performance and occupant health are the same case rather than two cases.

**The argument, from Yeang and Dilani's own Chapter I.** The book is explicit that it fuses two separately-founded bodies of work: it describes "Ken Yeang, who is the founder of ecological design, and Alan Dilani, the founder of salutogenic design principles," synthesising "nearly two decades of dialogue and interdisciplinary research."

The core chain, stated near-identically in the Acknowledgements and in Chapter I:

> "The challenge for ecological design is to provide a green context for a healthy society, dealing with built infrastructure that creates clean air, clean water, clean food, and clean land. This is achieved through water management and retention, natural heating and cooling, as well as renewable energy which, in turn, is necessary for human health and wellbeing. These principles are intertwined with those of salutogenic design, which support human health in daily life."

Note the mechanism is named: the four "cleans" are delivered by **water management and retention, natural heating and cooling, and renewable energy**. This is a direct bridge from bioclimatic building systems to health outcomes.

The division of labour between the two halves is stated crisply: "salutogenic design can provide social organisation, structure, and function in society; while ecological design can work to continually restore the natural environment."

Chapter I also grounds the health claim in specific evidence rather than assertion — "the link between access to natural light and blood pressure; between overcrowding or chronic noise and psychological stress; and the direct link between healing and nature" — and argues that greenery and walkable, open public space lower blood pressure, reduce risk of heart disease, stroke and diabetes, prevent falls in the elderly, and reduce mental fatigue and stress, "which may eventually reduce the costs of healthcare." That last clause is the business-case hook.

Framing anchors used: the WHO's 1997 identification of "the health arena" (workplaces, schools, hospitals, commercial offices, public spaces) as the centre of 21st-century health promotion; the UN General Assembly's 66th meeting (September 2011) taking up non-communicable diseases for the first time; and the UN **Sustainable Development Goals, especially Goal 3** (health and wellbeing for all), which the editors explicitly align the book with.

Dilani's theory, per the Note on Editors, "reflects Aaron Antonovsky's salutogenesis health theory," in which experiences that are comprehensible, manageable and meaningful build a **sense of coherence** that helps people mobilise resources against stressors. Dilani's claim is that design uninformed by salutogenic theory "causes unnecessary stress," so the design approach should be stress-preventive.

**A candid self-assessment worth knowing about.** The book's own Note on Editors says of Yeang's ambition to make buildings "run as complete ecosystems with little external energy supply": "**He is not there yet**, but the possibility of the green skyscraper is developing fast." Yeang's own publications repeatedly concede the theory-practice gap, which is worth knowing before a client raises it.

**Why it matters to this project.** It lands directly on the Burj Fitrah **Holistic** requirement and the health-across-five-systems framing. The health argument is not a bolt-on to the green argument: Yeang himself fused the two, in print, in 2021, co-editing with the founder of the salutogenic design field. The clean-air/water/food/land chain, and the named delivery mechanisms behind it, give a ready-made structure for arguing that environmental performance and occupant health are one case rather than two. Chapter VII (the salutogenic office) and Chapter VI (health-promoting urban design post-pandemic) are the most directly transferable to a mixed-use tower.

## RESOLVED: the *Bioclimatic Skyscrapers* edition tangle

Resolved against nine Open Library work records (openlibrary.org/search.json, query "bioclimatic skyscrapers yeang"), comparing publisher, place, page count, ISBN and listed contributors. **There are three distinct publications, not four or more.** The apparent multiplicity is duplicate cataloguing.

**1. *Bioclimatic Skyscrapers* — Artemis, London, 1994. THE BOOK.**
137 pages. ISBN 1874056560 / 9781874056560. A parallel German-market ISBN (3760884245 / 9783760884240) indicates an Artemis Verlag co-edition, and Rizzoli International appears as the US distributor. Contributors listed: **Ivor Richards** and **Alan Balfour** — so the essays are by others; this is a monograph on the practice's projects, not a solo treatise. Open Library work /works/OL1941184W.

**2. *Ken Yeang, Malaysia: Bioclimatic Skyscrapers* — Aedes, Berlin, 1994. AN EXHIBITION CATALOGUE, NOT A BOOK.**
60 pages, **no ISBN**, published by Aedes Galerie für Architektur und Raum, Berlin, with the same two contributors (Balfour, Richards). Open Library holds this under **three separate work records** (/works/OL38545995W "Ken Yeang, Malaysia, bioclimatic skyscrapers", /works/OL12404230W "Ken Yeang, Malaysia", /works/OL38713559W "Malaysia") — all Aedes, all Berlin, all 1994, all 60pp. These are **one catalogue triple-counted**. It accompanied an Aedes gallery exhibition and should not be counted in a book tally.

**3. *Bioclimatic Skyscrapers* — Ellipsis, London. A LATER RE-EDITION.**
Different publisher and pagination from the Artemis book (147–154pp vs 137pp), so a genuine re-edition rather than a reprint. Open Library splits it across two work records (/works/OL11983498W and /works/OL18908695W) which **share the identical ISBN 1899858709 / 9781899858705**, proving a single publication. The two records disagree on date: one says 1994, the other gives publish years 2000 and 1998. **The 1994 Ellipsis date is almost certainly a cataloguing error** (the shared ISBN and the second record's two-edition count point to 1998 with a 2000 reissue), but I flag the discrepancy rather than assert a date — **date UNVERIFIED, most probably 1998, reissued 2000.**

**Separately: *The Skyscraper Bioclimatically Considered: A Design Primer* is a genuinely DIFFERENT BOOK**, not an edition of the above. 1996, London, **269 pages** — roughly double the Artemis book. It too appears under two Open Library records, both 1996 and both 269pp, differing only in imprint and ISBN: **Academy Editions** (1854904310 / 9781854904317, /works/OL12404232W) and **Wiley-Academy** (0471977640 / 9780471977643, /works/OL1941198W). Academy Editions was absorbed into John Wiley, so these are **one book issued under two imprints**. Catalogue dates of 1997 and 1998 seen elsewhere are reprints of the same 269pp text.

**The substantive distinction to carry:** *Bioclimatic Skyscrapers* (1994) is a **project monograph with essays by Richards and Balfour**; *The Skyscraper Bioclimatically Considered* (1996) is a **design primer** — Yeang's own systematic technical statement, and the more serious of the two intellectually.

**Corrected count.** Of the "four or more" records, the defensible tally is **two books plus one exhibition catalogue plus one later re-edition**.

**Caution — a non-Yeang title that pollutes searches.** *The Bioclimatic Skyscraper* (singular) by **Puteri Shireen Jahn Kassim**, 2004, University of Brighton School of Architecture and Design (/works/OL11742839W) is a doctoral thesis by another author, not a Yeang publication. Note that Jahn Kassim later co-authored *Constructed Ecosystems* (2016) with Yeang, which makes the confusion easy.
